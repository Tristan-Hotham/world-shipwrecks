Wikipedia's volunteers have listed {{lists.n|int}} shipwrecks, year by year, since 1700. I counted them all. Three of the five worst years are war years, with {{lists.peak_year|raw}} the worst of all. But the biggest pile of wrecks comes from the 1800s, and that tells a story about British newspapers as much as about the sea.

Tucked away in English Wikipedia is one of the most patient pieces of volunteer history I know. For every month since the 1820s, and for every year or decade before, there is a page called "List of shipwrecks in..." Each one lists the ships lost, day by day: the name, the flag, and a line on what happened. "Driven ashore near Whitby." "Foundered in the North Sea; her crew were rescued." "Torpedoed and sunk by U-boat."

I turned all of those pages from 1700 to 2025 into one table. Once I had removed duplicates it held {{lists.n|int}} wrecks. Then I counted them, year by year, by cause and by flag.

Counts rise from almost nothing in the early 1700s to a long plateau, mostly between 2,000 and 3,800 a year from the 1840s to 1880, almost all ochre for ships wrecked or driven aground, then drop sharply to a few hundred a year. The wars

The two world wars stand out exactly as you would expect. The single worst year is {{lists.peak_year|raw}}, with {{lists.peak_n|int}} wrecks listed. Second is 1917, the year Germany turned to unrestricted submarine warfare, with {{lists.y1917|int}}, and third is 1942, with {{lists.y1942|int}}. Across the Second World War the lists average {{lists.ww2_avg|int}} wrecks a year, {{lists.ww2_vs_inter|f1}} times the rate between the wars, and {{lists.war_ww2_pct|pc}} of them were lost to war: torpedoes above all, then bombs from aircraft, mines and gunfire.

The age of sail, and Lloyd's List

The surprise is how close the 1800s come. From 1850 to 1879 the lists average {{lists.high19_avg|int}} wrecks a year, and in {{lists.peak19_year|raw}} they reach {{lists.peak19_n|int}}. {{lists.c19_pct|pc}} of all the entries are from the nineteenth century. These were mostly not dramatic losses: {{lists.c19_aground_pct|pc}} of them were ships that ran aground or were driven ashore in bad weather, and many crews got off safely.

Some of that is real. The sailing coasters and colliers of the 1800s were wrecked in enormous numbers. But a lot of it is the record. The Wikipedia editors who built these pages worked largely from British newspapers, and above all from Lloyd's List, which reported every British loss it heard about. {{lists.uk_c19_pct|pc}} of the 1800s entries are British ships. When the newspaper runs thin out, so do the lists: from 1890 to 1913 they average just {{lists.dip_avg|int}} wrecks a year. Shipping did get safer as steam replaced sail, but not that suddenly.

Whose ships

Over the whole period {{lists.uk_pct|pc}} of the listed wrecks flew the British flag, then the United States, Germany, France and Norway. In the Second World War the British share falls to {{lists.uk_ww2_pct|pc}}, as German, Japanese, Italian and Soviet losses join the lists.

{{IMG|b_flags.png|Stacked bar chart of the share of listed wrecks by flag per decade. Blue British ships make up about 60 to 85 percent of entries in most decades from the 1740s to the 1880s, then American red takes over much of the 1890s and 1900s; the 1940s show a wide mix including dark grey Germany and pink Japan, and recent decades are mostly other flags|Share of listed wrecks by flag, per decade.}}

Over everything, most ships were wrecked rather than sunk: {{lists.causes.2.pct|pc}} of the entries ran aground or were driven ashore, {{lists.causes.1.pct|pc}} foundered at sea, and {{lists.causes.0.pct|pc}} were lost to war.

The map

Most list entries have no position. The {{source.lists_points|int}} that do are mostly war losses, logged at the time, so they spread across the North Atlantic, the Mediterranean and the Pacific. I added every wreck in Wikidata and the United States wreck charts, for {{source.n|int}} wrecks with a place. The interactive map has a year slider with a play button, decade buttons, filters by cause and flag, and a sea-depth base map.

{{IMG|b_maps.png|A world map of shipwrecks on pale paper, with an enlarged panel of the British Isles. Red Second World War losses are scattered across the North Atlantic, the Mediterranean and the western Pacific; dense clusters crowd the coasts of Scotland, Wales and the eastern and Gulf coasts of the United States|Every mapped wreck, coloured by era of loss where a year is known (grey: undated).}}

The map has a bias of its own. {{coverage.uk_pct|pc}} of the mapped wrecks lie in UK waters, {{coverage.scotland_pct|pc}} off Scotland alone, because Wikidata imported the heritage records of Scotland and Wales wholesale. Scotland has {{coverage.england_per_scotland|f1}} times as many mapped wrecks as England, which certainly lost more ships.

How I did this

The lists come from a table scraped from every English Wikipedia "List of shipwrecks" page for 1700 to 2025, with ship, flag, a cause sorted into buckets and, for a minority, coordinates. I removed repeats of the same ship under the same date on the same page, grouped flags across regimes (the German Empire and Nazi Germany as Germany, for example) and grouped causes. For the map I added Wikidata's shipwrecks, with extra event dates, and NOAA's Automated Wreck and Obstruction Information System for US waters, and skipped list entries that matched a mapped wreck. I never guess a year from a launch date or a war. These are counts of recorded wrecks, not of all wrecks: the paper sets out every step and caveat.
